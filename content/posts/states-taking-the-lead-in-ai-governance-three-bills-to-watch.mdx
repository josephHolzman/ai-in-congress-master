The 10th Amendment to the U.S. The Constitution reserves powers not explicitly granted to the federal government to the states or the people. This holds enormous weight for artificial intelligence (AI) governance. AI governance is a framework of policies, procedures, and ethical considerations designed to guide the development and usage of AI systems. Primarily, it ensures that these systems are used in safe, ethical, and transparent ways that align with societal values and legal regulations.

Why does the 10th Amendment have to do with AI governance? The answer lies in the federal government’s current approach. The Trump administration has prioritized deregulation and a laissez-faire approach, and has sought to reduce federal oversight of AI development. [In a recent article](/posts/comparing-the-ai-policies-of-the-biden-and-trump-administrations), we compared the AI policies of the Biden and Trump administrations and found that President Trump has focused on encouraging AI innovation by scaling back regulatory measures. Notably, he rolled back several Biden-era AI directives through an Executive Order{/* TODO(joey): link */} on his first day in office.

This federal stance is further reinforced by the Republican majority in both the House and the Senate, which has largely aligned with the Trump administration’s stances. According to Nelson Mullins{/* TODO(joey): link */}, Senate Republicans favor a reserved approach to AI regulation, as they view it as a potential roadblock to innovation. However, they are also anticipated to prioritize AI-driven innovation, particularly in defense, national security, and economic growth.

With the federal government stepping back, the responsibility for AI governance is now more than ever on state lawmakers. In fact, it could be beneficial to state lawmakers who want more AI protections. As AI becomes more integrated into daily life, states are poised to play an increasingly vital role in shaping policies that ensure ethical and responsible AI use while balancing the need for innovation. Below, we will review three significant state-level AI governance bills that have been introduced since the beginning of the Trump administration and the 119th Congress. ## California Assembly Bill 1018 - Preventing AI Discrimination

AB 1018{/* TODO(joey): link */} is California’s most ambitious attempt yet to regulate AI-driven decision-making in areas like employment, education, housing, and healthcare. The bill aims to protect individuals from algorithmic bias by requiring developers and deployers of AI systems to conduct impact assessments and ensure their models do not disproportionately harm protected groups. If passed, this legislation would set a precedent for AI accountability, making California a leader in AI fairness regulation.

AB 1018 was introduced in direct response to the Trump administration’s rollback of federal AI regulations. On his first day in office, President Trump signed an executive order repealing several Biden-era directives that had established guidelines for AI transparency and fairness. With the federal government stepping back, California lawmakers sought to fill the gap by enacting stronger state-level protections against AI-driven discrimination. This bill reflects a broader trend in which states, particularly those with Democratic leadership, are moving to regulate AI independently as the federal government shifts toward a more laissez-faire approach.

## Connecticut Senate Bill 2 - Establishing AI Oversight and Transparency

Connecticut’s SB 2{/* TODO(joey): link */} is a comprehensive AI governance proposal that seeks to introduce greater transparency and accountability in the private sector. The bill mandates that companies deploying AI systems disclose their use and provide individuals with explanations of how automated decisions impact them. Additionally, SB 2 calls for an independent AI oversight body to evaluate risks associated with AI and ensure compliance with ethical standards. If enacted, this law could serve as a model for other states looking to regulate AI without stifling innovation.

SB 2 emerged as a direct reaction to the Trump administration’s hands-off approach to AI regulation. With the federal government scaling back its role, Connecticut lawmakers recognized the growing risks posed by unregulated AI in financial services, hiring, and public services. The state legislature positioned SB 2 as a safeguard against AI-driven economic inequalities, particularly in sectors where automated decision-making can have lasting consequences for individuals.

## Texas House Bill 1709 - Regulating Automated AI Decision-Making

Texas’ HB 1709{/* TODO(joey): link */} focuses on the growing use of automated decision-making systems in high-stakes scenarios, such as credit approvals, hiring, and law enforcement. The bill would require AI developers to register certain automated decision-making tools and allow consumers to appeal decisions made by AI. By introducing measures to prevent AI-related harm while fostering a business-friendly environment, Texas is positioning itself as a state that seeks to balance regulation with technological advancement.

While Texas generally leans toward deregulation, HB 1709 was introduced in response to concerns that the federal government’s withdrawal from AI oversight could leave workers vulnerable to biased and opaque hiring algorithms. By taking a state-level approach, Texas lawmakers acknowledged the risks AI poses in employment while maintaining a business-friendly regulatory framework. The bill reflects a growing bipartisan understanding that AI in hiring needs at least some level of oversight to ensure fair labor practices.

## The Path Forward

State lawmakers are now at the forefront of AI governance, shaping policies that address the risks and opportunities of artificial intelligence. California, Connecticut, and Texas have each taken unique approaches to AI regulation, reflecting the broader national debate over balancing innovation and accountability. As more states introduce similar legislation, the question remains: Will this patchwork of state-level regulations push the federal government to step back in, or will AI governance continue to evolve on a state-by-state basis?
